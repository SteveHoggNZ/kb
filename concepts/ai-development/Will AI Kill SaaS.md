# Will AI Kill SaaS?

Einar Vollset's thesis (presented by Tracy Osborn at MicroConf New Orleans): AI will kill *some* SaaS — the kind with simple objective functions — but far from all of it, and the disruption is a massive opportunity rather than an extinction event.

The argument is structured as a debate: the bear case ("the Twitter case for death") answered by four reasons SaaS survives. The most load-bearing of those is the one about **infinite human desire** — the same abundance logic as [[The Great Inversion]] and the value-migration story in [[The Barbell Economy]].

---

## The case for death

- **Today:** code is far cheaper and easier to write, for many more people. Companies assemble custom internal tools and cancel subscriptions.
- **In 1–2 years:** AI automates SaaS *tasks* directly — "Optimize marketing," "Analyze Q2" — instead of "give me a SaaS to manage FB spend" or "build me a dashboard."
- **In 3–5 years:** **invisible software** — software as we know it vanishes as AI solves business problems end-to-end.

## The case for resurrection (four arguments)

**1. AI capability improvements are not accelerating.** Recent gains came from *more compute + more data*, not algorithmic breakthroughs ("bigger is better"). But there's little internet text left to train on, and the **neural scaling power law** means each added resource buys progressively smaller gains. (The same supply/scaling constraint as [[The Inference Crunch]] — physical inputs, not magic, drive the curve.)

**2. Democratizing the means of production ≠ automating excellence/creativity.** There are more possible books than atoms in the observable universe; a model can't write them all, and even if it could it couldn't tell which is the masterpiece — because **masterpieces violate expectations in exactly the right way**. Taste is not a computable objective. (This is [[The Barbell Economy|Layer 2]] / [[Seven Dimensions of Hard Work|the human dimensions]]: judgment, taste, ambiguity.)

**3. Software complexity mirrors societal complexity — and human desire may be infinite.** ⭐ Human desires, problems, and economic activity have **no clear upper bound**. Software complexity mirrors societal complexity, and *cheaper* software development lets us **traverse deeper into that (possibly infinite) complexity** — more software, more economic efficiency, more opportunity. **Jevon's Paradox** applies: making software cheaper to build increases the total amount of software demanded, not less. Falling execution cost doesn't shrink the market — it expands the frontier, the optimistic face of [[The Great Inversion]]'s "when you remove a bottleneck it moves" and [[Scarcity to Abundance - A Unifying Lens|scarcity → abundance]].

**4. We're (probably) not creating God.** Most real-world problems, unlike chess, **lack a clear, unchanging, computable objective function**; they branch infinitely, need nuanced/intuitive judgment, and require iterative human interaction bound by the "interface speed" of human cognition. Only a god-like entity could predict the future and know what you can't yet express — for everyone, in every situation, forever.

## What they believe

- AI will **quickly wipe out SaaS with simple objective functions.**
- **Human creativity, empathy, connection, and understanding** become far more important than mastery of "ritualistic practices" (specific engineering techniques) — echoing [[The Great Inversion]]'s point that relationships are the durable moat ("you cannot vibecode a relationship").
- **Incumbents are more vulnerable than ever.**
- **But that's good — massive opportunity:** expect **5–10 person teams with $100M ARR, very soon.** *"Go forth! Create! What a time to be alive!"*

That tiny-team / huge-outcome prediction is the commercial payoff of [[Small Teams]] and [[The Great Inversion]]: when execution is cheap, a handful of people with taste and distribution can capture what once took a department.

---

## The through-line

The bear case and bull case agree on the mechanism — execution is collapsing in cost — and disagree only on what that *does* to the market. Vollset's wager is that cheap execution doesn't saturate demand; it **unlocks** it (Jevon's Paradox on infinite human desire), so value migrates from "the software" to the scarce human inputs around it: taste, judgment, empathy, and distribution. SaaS with a simple objective function dies; everything requiring [[Seven Dimensions of Hard Work|non-computable judgment]] becomes *more* valuable. It's [[The Barbell Economy]] told as a founder's growth story rather than a warning.

## Connection to Other Concepts

### [[The Barbell Economy]]
Same value-migration thesis: contestable/tokenizable work (simple-objective SaaS) collapses toward zero; judgment, accountability, and relationships (Layer 2) become the premium. This note is the optimistic, entrepreneurial framing of it.

### [[The Great Inversion]]
Cheap execution moves the bottleneck to clarity, ambition, distribution, and relationships. "5–10 person, $100M ARR" is that inversion's commercial headline; Jevon's Paradox is why the pie grows rather than shrinks.

### [[The Inference Crunch]]
Argument 1 (scaling has diminishing returns, data is running out) rests on the same physical-constraint reality — compute, data, and power bound the curve.
